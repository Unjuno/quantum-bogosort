\section{Related Work}

The QBS framework sits at the intersection of several established literatures. Everettian decision-theoretic work derives or interprets Born-rule weights under rationality assumptions, including Wallace's formal decision-theoretic proof \cite{wallace2009born} and Greaves's caring-measure interpretation \cite{greaves2004deutsch}. Work by Sebens and Carroll develops self-locating uncertainty as a basis for Everettian credence \cite{sebenscarroll2018selflocating}, while Saunders develops accounts of chance, branch counting, and physical probability in branching theories \cite{saunders2016chance,saunders2021probability,saunders2021branchcounting,saunders2026physicalprobability}.

The status of self-locating probability in Everett remains contested. Lewis argues that branching structure by itself may be insufficient to determine self-locating probabilities and that decision-theoretic arguments do not automatically resolve that problem \cite{lewis2009selflocation}. More recently, Khawaja develops indexed branch-counting as a rationally motivated self-locating credence rule that can diverge from the Born rule \cite{khawaja2026branchcounting}. These results reinforce the present manuscript's treatment of the Everett accessibility map as a separate bridge assumption rather than as a consequence of branching alone.

The broader decision-theoretic program is also contested. Price argues that the analogy with ordinary decision under uncertainty is not automatic \cite{price2006probability,price2008decisions}, while Kent emphasizes that branch weights, rational decision roles, and empirical confirmation are logically distinct problems \cite{kent2009oneworld}. Ara\'ujo's comparison of deterministic many-world theories likewise illustrates that the relevant probability measure can depend on the underlying dynamics \cite{araujo2018deterministic}. These critiques support the present manuscript's decision to keep the abstract first-person change of measure separate from any physical Everett identification.

Quantum-suicide discussions provide historical background for extreme observer-selection intuitions \cite{tegmark1997manywords}, but Many-Worlds alone does not establish certain survival or quantum immortality \cite{mallah2009immortality}. This manuscript therefore treats such thought experiments as historical background rather than as consequences of the QBS theorems.

Observer-selection proposals such as Hanson's mangled-worlds mechanism are closer to a physical selection model \cite{hanson2003mangled}, but their mechanism differs sharply from the abstract accessibility map used here. Garisto's general observer-selection formalism is also relevant because it shows that observer-selection effects can depend strongly on how the selection protocol and reference sets are specified \cite{garisto2020selectobservers}. This supports the QBS requirement that any physical or anthropic interpretation of $S$ state its selection assumptions explicitly rather than treating observer weighting as automatic.

Anthropically motivated decision theories, including Armstrong's anthropic decision theory \cite{armstrong2011anthropic}, provide another nearby literature in which policy choice is studied under self-locating structure. There is also direct recent overlap between self-locating beliefs and policy optimization. Cooper, Oesterheld, and Conitzer study whether modified anthropic/self-locating beliefs can make causal decision theory recover ex ante optimal policies in Newcomb-like problems \cite{cooperoesterheldconitzer2024anthropics}. Conitzer separately studies the extent to which rational-choice behavior can adjudicate de se beliefs and cautions that action quality may depend on features other than the self-locating probabilities themselves \cite{conitzer2015dese}. These works mean that QBS cannot claim novelty merely for connecting self-location, agent copies, and policy-level action choice.

The normalized weighting operation itself also belongs to a broad classical change-of-measure and importance-sampling literature; weighted empirical measures are standard objects in that setting \cite{hultnyquist2016importance}. Accordingly, neither normalized weighting, the law of total covariance, nor the elementary covariance identity is presented as the central novelty of QBS.

The provisional novelty claim is therefore narrower: QBS makes policy explicitly recognition-dependent, including the case where recognition concerns a QBS-type rule itself; allows the resulting policy to change both trajectory utility and accessibility; gives exact decompositions of ordinary trajectory effects, first-person conditioning effects, policy--selection interaction, and general-accessibility residual dependence; models predictor/accessibility alignment endogenously in adaptive toy agents; and explicitly distinguishes single-observer marginal uplift from branch-wide policy coherence. A supplementary shared-latent theorem characterizes one condition under which cross-copy action covariance is nonnegative. This positioning remains subject to revision if more direct prior art is found.
