# Recognition-follow-up longitudinal protocol

This unnumbered supplementary protocol specifies how a recognition-dependent observer model could be investigated through the later sequence of observations without assuming that the future was fixed in advance. It is not T6, S2.14, E6, or evidence for an Everett/QBS physical bridge.

## H — hypothesis and temporal direction

Let `H_t` denote the information/history available by time `t`, and let `I_t` denote a latent observer/continuation state in a candidate model.

The model considered here is **past-adapted** in the following sense:

```math
I_{t+1}
\sim
K_t(\cdot\mid I_t,H_t).
```

The transition rule can depend on the past and present state but not on future realized outcomes.

After the latent state is realized, the later sequence remains unrealized and is generated according to the corresponding conditional law:

```math
Y_{t+1:T}
\sim
P(\cdot\mid H_t,I_{t+1}).
```

Thus the intended direction is

```math
(H_t,I_t)
\longrightarrow
I_{t+1}
\longrightarrow
\mathcal L(Y_{t+1:T}\mid H_t,I_{t+1})
\longrightarrow
Y_{t+1:T}.
```

The protocol does **not** assume that the realized future sequence is specified, selected, or determined at time `t`.

## Recognition and later sequence

The empirical question is not whether an individual future trajectory was known beforehand. It is whether recognition and the associated history-dependent observer dynamics are followed by a longitudinal sequence whose **distributional structure** differs from suitable null models.

Let `R_t` denote recognition/adoption state. A candidate model may imply

```math
\mathcal L(Y_{t+1:T}\mid H_t,R_t=1)
\neq
\mathcal L_0(Y_{t+1:T}\mid H_t,R_t=1),
```

where `L_0` is the law generated by an ordinary behavioral, selection, or continuity null.

This is a statement about distributions over later sequences, not about a single future being fixed in advance.

## Two different empirical questions

Two study designs answer different questions and should not be conflated.

### 1. Natural-recognition longitudinal study

Follow people from the time they genuinely recognize or adopt the framework and characterize the later sequence of outcomes, behavior, information, and observation availability.

This design can reveal longitudinal signatures but is vulnerable to baseline differences between people who do and do not recognize/adopt the framework.

### 2. Recognition-exposure experiment

Where ethically and practically possible, exposure to the framework can be externally assigned and compared with a matched control exposure.

This design targets the **causal effect of exposure/recognition opportunity**, not the ontology itself. It can help separate ordinary expectancy, behavior, risk-taking, and information effects from any additional observer-model signature.

Random assignment is therefore useful for causal isolation, but it is not a metaphysical requirement of the model and it does not make the future sequence predetermined.

## Sequential distributional comparison

The later sequence can be analyzed with ordinary longitudinal and sequential statistics. Depending on the model class, this may include:

- conditional transition frequencies;
- survival/attrition-aware sequence models;
- hidden-state or state-space models;
- likelihood-ratio or predictive-density comparison;
- held-out replication across people, periods, or contexts;
- calibration of distributional summaries rather than exact-path prediction.

If a candidate observer model and a null supply conditional laws `P_{1,t}` and `P_{0,t}`, a cumulative log-likelihood ratio can be used:

```math
L_T
=
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
\log
\frac{dP_{1,t}}{dP_{0,t}}(Y_{t+1}).
```

This does not mean `Y_{t+1}` was fixed beforehand. It compares how well two stochastic models account for the sequence once observations arrive.

The models may be estimated on one part of the data and checked on another, or compared through other reproducible procedures. The essential requirement is not advance determination of the future; it is that the model class have enough structure that unfavorable data can count against it.

## Separating ordinary policy effects from innovation

Recognition can alter ordinary behavior. A favorable post-recognition trajectory is therefore not by itself evidence for observer selection.

Relative to an information filtration, decompose

```math
m_{t+1}
=
E[Y_{t+1}\mid H_t],
\qquad
\varepsilon_{t+1}
=
Y_{t+1}-m_{t+1}.
```

Then

```math
A_T
=
\sum_t m_{t+1},
\qquad
M_T
=
\sum_t \varepsilon_{t+1}.
```

The longitudinal analysis should distinguish as far as possible:

- changes in `A_T` attributable to ordinary policy, effort, information, risk exposure, or treatment effects;
- changes in the distribution of `M_T`, the variation left unpredictable relative to the chosen information set.

If only the predictable component changes, ordinary policy improvement may be sufficient. A stronger observer-accessibility interpretation requires additional structure in the residual or sequential law that competing ordinary models do not reproduce.

Innovation is filtration-relative. Different information sets can move variation between predictable and innovation components; this must be treated as a modeling choice rather than a physically privileged decomposition.

## Required competing explanations

At minimum, analysis should consider alternatives such as:

1. baseline differences among people who recognize/adopt the framework;
2. ordinary history-dependent policy adaptation;
3. equivalent-information Bayesian/RL learning;
4. expectancy/placebo effects;
5. changes in effort, risk-taking, or information acquisition;
6. survivorship, attrition, missingness, and reporting selection;
7. classical ascertainment or record-selection models;
8. a candidate observer-accessibility model only to the extent that it adds constraints not freely reproducible by those alternatives.

The point is not to declare one model true from a favorable sequence, but to compare constrained explanations of the longitudinal law.

## Attrition and survivorship

Loss to follow-up, nonresponse, death, incapacitation, selective reporting, or retrospective exclusion can produce exactly the kind of selected sequence that might otherwise be misread as an observer-selection effect.

Therefore longitudinal analysis should:

- retain the original observation population where possible;
- report missingness and attrition explicitly;
- avoid discarding unfavorable or incomplete trajectories merely because they are difficult to classify;
- distinguish unavailable records from favorable/neutral/unfavorable outcomes;
- model observation probability when it plausibly depends on post-recognition state;
- include survivorship-only classical explanations among the primary nulls.

A survivorship-selected dataset cannot by itself identify an Everett/QBS mechanism.

## Consciousness gaps and hidden transitions

Periods without reportable conscious observation, such as ordinary sleep, are especially relevant because more than one latent continuation can be compatible with the same observable record at the end of the gap.

Suppose two latent post-gap states satisfy the same currently observed history but imply different later laws:

```math
H_t^{(1)}=H_t^{(2)},
```

while

```math
\mathcal L(Y_{t+1:T}\mid H_t,I^{(1)}_t)
\neq
\mathcal L(Y_{t+1:T}\mid H_t,I^{(2)}_t).
```

Then the hidden transition itself is not directly observed, yet later longitudinal data may discriminate between transition **models** at the population or repeated-sequence level.

This does not permit the statement that a particular favorable outcome proves that a switch occurred. It means only that an unobserved transition can have observable downstream statistical consequences.

## What prevents an anything-goes theory

The model remains constrained even though the future path is not fixed in advance.

A usable candidate model must specify enough of the following to generate nontrivial restrictions:

- latent state space;
- history-dependent transition/accessibility kernel;
- support and normalization;
- relation between latent state and later observable law;
- cross-context or cross-person invariances where claimed;
- observation/attrition process;
- parameters or parameter families with finite effective flexibility.

A model that simply assigns a new latent explanation to every realized trajectory after observing it has no identifying content.

Falsification can therefore come from failure of these structural restrictions, failure to reproduce replicated longitudinal regularities, or inferior fit/prediction relative to comparably flexible null models. None of this requires the exact future realization to be predetermined.

## C — current conclusion

The framework need not be purely retrospective or metaphysical. A past-dependent latent observer transition can be unobservable when it occurs and still alter the conditional distribution of later experience.

The empirical target is therefore:

```math
\text{recognition/history}
\longrightarrow
\text{latent transition/accessibility law}
\longrightarrow
\text{later sequence distribution},
```

not

```math
\text{future favorable outcome}
\longrightarrow
\text{retroactively chosen earlier transition}.
```

The future itself remains open at the transition time.

## U — unresolved / non-claims

- A favorable post-recognition sequence does not by itself prove observer selection.
- Recognition may change ordinary behavior, beliefs, effort, risk, and information acquisition.
- Failure of one classical null does not uniquely identify Everett.
- Failure to distinguish latent continuity descriptions during sleep or another consciousness gap does not prove branch switching.
- Later sequence data can support or disfavor a transition model statistically without causing the earlier transition.
- No claim is made that human consciousness causes branching, controls branch choice, changes objective quantum probabilities, or guarantees favorable continuation.

## Error check

The temporal restriction is **causal**, not deterministic: the latent transition depends only on past/current state, while the future sequence remains unrealized. The theory becomes empirically interesting only if different allowed latent transitions induce different later observable laws; otherwise the alternatives remain observationally equivalent.
